# Red team: ventures/glp1-companion/brief.md (C11)
Date: 2026-11-15 | Verdict: **needs work** (the brief and its test plan) / **fail as a venture case** (wedge (b) "after the shot" as a seed-fundable company)

Mode RED TEAM, task C13 second half. First red team of this brief. Facts are taken as checked in `research/reviews/2026-11-15-glp1-companion-brief.md`; its points (one price, pass mark per €150 theme, R2 kill line, minor source fixes) are not repeated. No new sources were opened; every number below is from the brief or the fact-check, and every calculation is marked *my math*. The fix round for this brief is task C14 (rule 14); its description already says to apply this review. No new fix task is added.

**Summary for the CEO.**
- The brief is honest. Its own finding is already close to a kill: the "companion" is given away free by the drug sellers, and the market is small (€18m a year in Germany at full penetration).
- **The test it recommends would not test the idea.** The ads may not name the drug, so they reach anyone who wants to keep weight off, not people who stop the drug. One of the two ad themes ("low appetite") targets people still on the drug, which is the wedge the brief rejected. A pass would show general interest in weight maintenance, where Weight Watchers sells for €11.25 a month (F2).
- **The money maths is too kind.** The product lasts 12 weeks, but revenue assumes customers pay for 6 months. With the 12-week length, each customer brings €83–129, and only €32–59 may be spent to win one (F3).
- **Anyone can copy it, and someone already has.** A one-person site (nachderspritze.de) describes the same programme point by point. The founder's AI skill is not what decides success; advertising skill in a restricted health category is (F4).
- **My recommendation for C12 (you decide): (d) drop the GLP-1 companion as a venture.** If you want to learn what it costs to win a paying consumer at all, which also matters for the midlife-women theme, approve a test only in the redesigned form below (pre-order with payment, one theme, target group reachable). Do not approve the test as written in section 13, even after C14.

## Fatal or near-fatal objections

**F1. Near-fatal: the target customer just left a product because it cost too much, and the brief asks them to pay again for the outcome it may not promise.**
- Section 3 defines wedge (b) as people "who stopped or are tapering a self-paid GLP-1 drug, plus those who are about to stop because of cost". Section 2 shows cost and side effects as the stop reasons (26.8 % adverse reaction, 14.4 % cost; US mixed cohort, per fact-check not a DE self-payer figure).
- The value is "regain less weight". Section 1 says "**Unproven:** no source shows that such a programme reduces regain after stopping", and section 4 rules out a "weight-loss promise". So the ad, the landing page and the product cannot state the one result the buyer wants. Section 13 theme A ("Gewicht halten nach dem Abnehmen") comes close; whether that wording is allowed for a service aimed at people leaving an obesity drug is not examined (see F5).
- The bifg row says ~16 % stop "after the first use". *Inference:* many stoppers lost little weight and have little to "keep". The real target is a smaller group: people who lost a lot and then stopped. No number exists for it; section 5 leaves SAM "not counted".
- **Buyer (a person who just stopped Mounjaro at €300+ a month):** "I stopped because I could not afford it. Now you want €129 for recipes, a training plan and a chatbot. WW is €11.25 a month on the 12-month deal, YouTube is free, the GLP-1 forum has a free 'Absetzen' section, and my gym already has a trainer. If I regain anyway, I will ask for my money back."

**F2. Fatal for the recommended option (b): the demand test as designed cannot tell the brief's hypothesis apart from generic diet demand.**
- Section 13: "No drug names, no 'GLP-1', no 'Abnehmspritze' unless the lawyer hour clears it." Without the drug name, ad platforms cannot be told "people who stopped the injection". Theme A then reaches everyone who wants to keep weight off. That is WW's market (€11.25–45), not wedge (b).
- Theme B, "Genug Eiweiß, wenn der Appetit fehlt", describes people **on** the drug (low appetite is a drug effect). After stopping, appetite returns (*inference*). Theme B tests wedge (a), which section 4 calls "not recommended: the drug platforms already bundle it free".
- So the possible results all mislead: a pass on A = generic maintenance interest (the brief cannot claim that segment against WW); a pass on B = interest in the wedge the brief already rejected; a fail = could be the creative, the platform's weight-loss ad limits (`[UNVERIFIED]` in section 8), or the price.
- A waitlist e-mail is not a payment. The pass mark rests on "10 % of price-aware waitlist sign-ups later pay (*assumption*)". That assumption is the unknown that decides the case, and the test does not measure it. ~20 sign-ups per theme (after C14) gives 2 expected payers, too few to read anything.
- **Investor:** "€300 of Meta ads with a waitlist tells me your creative works on dieters. It tells me nothing about the payback period on a GLP-1 leaver."
- Fix for C14 (if the test stays at all): one theme only, aimed at the stated segment; the target group reached by a channel that does not need the drug name in the ad (see Cheapest tests, T3); a pre-order with payment and a full refund if the cohort does not start, instead of a waitlist; pass mark = paid pre-orders, not e-mails. A pre-order needs a seller (Impressum, payment account) and is an outward action for the CEO to approve; say so.

**F3. Near-fatal: the revenue per customer assumes 6 months of a 12-week product.**
- Section 4 defines "a 12-week digital programme". Section 5 assumes "Average paid life **6 months** = **€180 per customer**". Section 7 names the repeat driver as "the programme length (12 weeks)" and "possibly a restart when a person goes back on the drug". So a renewal is either a second 12-week run with no new content, or a return to the drug, which takes the customer out of wedge (b).
- *My math*, using the brief's own 3-month case and the two prices in the brief:
  - €30/month × 3 = €90; allowed acquisition cost at 39–46 % = **€35–41** (the brief's own figure).
  - €129 per 12 weeks once = €129; allowed = **€50–59** (0.39 × 129 = 50.3; 0.46 × 129 = 59.3).
  - At €7.50 per sign-up (the pass mark) the waitlist must convert **13–15 %** (7.50 / 59.3; 7.50 / 50.3) at €129, or **18–21 %** at €90, not the assumed 10 %.
  - €1m revenue at €129 needs **~7,750 new customers a year** (1,000,000 / 129), ~7.8 % of the 2024 self-payer pool every year.
- The 39–46 % marketing benchmark comes from Hims & Hers and PharmaSGP: companies with repeat purchases (drug subscriptions, pharmacy re-orders) and known brands. A one-off programme from an unknown brand has neither (*inference*). The fact-check flagged this as a red-team question; it cuts against the brief.
- C14 should re-derive section 5 and the pass mark on a 12-week life unless it can name a sourced reason for renewals.

**F4. Near-fatal for founder fit and for any investor: there is no moat, and the decisive skill is missing.**
- `founder-brief.md`: strengths are health-innovation training and AI agent implementation; gaps are clinical and engineering. The consumer scan gave fit 5 "because the product is mostly coaching and automation". But the brief itself says acquisition cost "decides the case" (section 11). Neither the CEO nor any named person has performance marketing or nutrition credentials. Both are listed as "needed"; R7 waits to 31 Mar 2027 for one candidate.
- AI coaching is not a difference: Juniper already has a "KI-Chat" (its weakness was missing history data, a bug, not a moat), WW ships protein targets and strength videos, and the fact-check found that nachderspritze.de's programme "matches section 4 point by point". **Anyone could do this, and someone has.** That is a finding, not a risk.
- Distribution sits with the platforms. GoLighter (Wellster, profitable per press, €50m revenue per press) holds the customer's data and e-mail at the moment of stopping. If leavers are worth anything, the cheapest move for GoLighter is to keep the WW programme on after the drug order ends, or to sell a paid maintenance tier. The brief's gap is a setting in someone else's checkout (*inference*).
- Low-dose or tablet maintenance (Wegovy tablet from ~€170) is a medical alternative to "stopping". Section 2 sees cheaper drugs as shrinking wedge (b) only through cost-driven stops; it misses that physicians and platforms will offer "stay on a lower dose" to the same person (*inference*).
- Location is neutral to slightly positive: a DTC programme can be built part-time from Lisbon, and German-language content is a small edge. Munich adds nothing specific except the Wellster alumni pool (section 11), who are more likely to compete than join.
- **Investor (Munich seed):** "Germany-only TAM of €18m at 100 % penetration; one-off €129 tickets; a product a nutritionist with Canva can copy; free bundles from a profitable local platform and a listed brand; your exit buyer gets it free from WW. I would not take the meeting. Show me a payer, a moat (proprietary outcome data, an exclusive platform contract), or repeat revenue."

**F5. Serious: the regulatory reading is narrower than the product's positioning.**
- Section 8 checks HWG only for **drug** ads and MDR only for **dosing** features. It concludes that a programme "without a medical purpose is *probably* not a medical device" and notes "a claim to treat obesity or prevent regain as a disease outcome could change that".
- But wedge (b) is positioned **only** around a disease treatment: the name ("Nach der Spritze"), the target group (people leaving an obesity drug) and the value (prevent regain) all point at it. Intended purpose is read from the marketing as a whole (*inference*; MDCG text not re-read, it was offline). The brief's own design choice makes the risky reading more likely, not less.
- Whether HWG reaches advertising for a non-drug programme aimed at an obesity-treatment context is not asked. The lawyer question in section 8 is too narrow ("may ads name Abnehmspritze users"). The useful question is: "may a coaching service positioned for people stopping an obesity drug advertise weight maintenance at all, and is that a medical purpose under MDR?" `[UNVERIFIED]` until answered.
- AI chat for people who just stopped an obesity drug (many with diabetes or other conditions, *inference*) needs safety limits and a human professional. Section 4 has "escalation to a human nutrition professional"; section 5 has "20 % COGS". Nobody priced the human. *My math:* at €129 per customer and 20 % COGS = ~€26 per customer for hosting, AI and a professional's time over 12 weeks.

**F6. Method: the 37/55 score was given to a different product and would not hold today.**
- The consumer scan scored row 5 "GLP-1 self-payers (protein, muscle, hair, coaching)", i.e. wedge (a) during treatment, with Competition 3M, Evidence 4H ("lean-mass problem [A]") and Fit 5M.
- The brief changed the facts under three of those scores: competition is free bundles plus WW at €11.25 (the scan's own rule gives 1–2 where a strong incumbent owns the job); the evidence for wedge (b) is "Unproven" (section 1) and the muscle-loss hook is "no worse than with dieting"; fit is weaker per F4.
- *My math:* Competition 3 → 1 (−2), Evidence 4 → 2 (−2), Fit 5 → 3 (−2) gives **31/55**. That is below every other top-5 row (36–38) and below the migraine reference row (34) that failed K1. The ranks 1–4 tie at 37–38 shows how one optimistic weight-1 score can lift a row into the top 5. Additive scoring let a row with Spend 2M (no companion spend found) rank 4th on Fit and Evidence that belonged to another wedge.
- The brief's header still says "Score: 37/55" without a note. C14 should add one line: "Score is for wedge (a); wedge (b) not re-scored; competition and evidence would fall."

## Pre-mortem and kill criteria
Assume it is November 2028 and the company has closed. The five most likely reasons, checked against the brief's evidence:

| # | Cause of death | Evidence in the brief today | Kill criterion (dated observable) |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | **Paid customers cost more than they bring.** Ads could not name the segment; generic weight-maintenance ads competed with WW; one-off €129 tickets | No category CAC (section 5, Gaps); 39–46 % benchmark is from repeat-purchase companies (F3) | Pre-order test (T3): fewer than **6 paid pre-orders** at €129 for €300 ad spend, i.e. more than €50 per paying customer, the low end of the €50–59 allowance. *My threshold.* |
| P2 | **The platforms closed the gap.** GoLighter kept WW free for leavers or sold a maintenance tier; Hims/Juniper added a "stop safely" module | GoLighter extras tied to a drug order is an *inference* from one sentence (section 6) | Before any spend (T1, desk, now): any one of GoLighter, Juniper, Voy or WW offers a maintenance or "after the drug" programme to leavers at ≤€15/month or free. The brief's R3 needs "≥2 of 3"; one is enough, since WW alone is open to anyone |
| P3 | **Customers did not stay or come back.** 12 weeks, no renewal; outcome (regain) not controllable; refunds and bad reviews | Section 1 "Unproven"; section 7 repeat driver is the programme length itself | Pilot cohort: **<30 % of completers buy a second period** at full price within 4 weeks of finishing. *My threshold* |
| P4 | **Regulatory stop.** A competitor or Wettbewerbszentrale warning letter on "weight maintenance after the Abnehmspritze", or the programme is read as a medical device | Section 8 examines drug ads and dosing only (F5) | Lawyer hour (wider question in F5): no compliant wording that names either the drug context or weight maintenance |
| P5 | **No team, no time.** No nutrition co-founder; no marketer; CEO full-time only from Sep 2027; nachderspritze.de or WW launches first | R7 "No named candidate" | **31 Mar 2027**: no named nutrition co-founder **and** no named performance marketer willing to work for equity (both, not one) |

Interview kill criteria (section 13) need tightening. "≥3 of 7 patients have already paid for a companion product or service outside the drug" counts a gym membership or a protein tub, so it will pass almost always. Confirm only if **≥3 of 7 paid ≥€50 for structured coaching or a programme aimed at keeping weight off** (*my threshold*). Kill if **≥4 of 7 say they would go back on the drug (or a lower dose) rather than buy a programme**.

## Fixes for C14 (the one fix round; rule 14)
1. Section 5: re-derive on a 12-week product life (one-off €129, or 3 × €30) unless a sourced renewal driver is given; restate the CAC allowance (F3).
2. Section 13: either replace the test with T3 (one theme, segment reachable, paid pre-order), or state plainly that the current test measures generic weight-maintenance demand and drop theme B (F2).
3. Section 8: widen the lawyer question to HWG for non-drug programmes in an obesity-treatment context and to MDR intended purpose from positioning (F5).
4. Section 12: replace R3 "≥2 of 3" with "any one incumbent, WW included"; add P3 (renewal) and the tightened interview criteria.
5. Header: note that 37/55 scores wedge (a) and would fall for wedge (b) (F6).
6. Bottom line: add that the idea has no moat (nachderspritze.de is the same programme) and that the founder's decisive gap is marketing, not product (F4).
